**The last word of the book**

Many chapters of the Tao Te Ching come back to one short Chinese phrase: 不爭 (bu zheng), "not contending" or "not competing." It even closes the book. The last line of chapter 81 says: "The Tao of the Nameless acts and does not compete."

**Why no one can compete with him**

Chapter 22 describes the sage:

*Not boasting, he is credited.*
*Not claiming, he endures.*
*Because he does not compete,*
*no one can compete with him.*

Chapter 66 repeats the last two lines, after showing how rivers and seas become "kings of the hundred valleys" by staying low. Whoever is not in the race cannot be beaten in it.

**Like water**

Chapter 8 says [water](/glossary/water/) "serves myriad things and never competes," and ends: "Do not fight, and no one can blame you." Not contending is water's way: useful, low and patient.

**A good fighter is not angry**

Chapter 68 brings the idea into conflict itself:

*A good soldier is not violent.*
*A good fighter is not angry.*
*A good conqueror does not engage.*
*This is the virtue of not contending.*

The angry fighter is easy to move. The calm one is not. This connects not contending with [softness](/glossary/softness/) and with [Te](/glossary/te/), the quiet power the book calls virtue.

**Not passivity**

Chapter 73 is clear that this is not giving up: "The way of heaven does not strive, yet wins." Chapter 81 adds: "The good do not argue." You still act, but you stop spending yourself on rivalry, scorekeeping and winning arguments.
