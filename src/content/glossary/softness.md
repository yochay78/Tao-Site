**The quality of living things**

Softness is one of the Tao Te Ching's quiet main ideas. The Chinese word is 柔 (rou): soft, supple, yielding. The book keeps pairing it with "weak," which surprises many readers. But it does not mean feeble. It means alive and able to bend.

**Followers of life**

Chapter 76 says it most clearly:

*A person is soft and weak at birth,*
*hard and stiff at death.*
*So the hard and stiff are followers of death.*
*The soft and weak are followers of life.*

A young branch bends in the wind. A dry one snaps. "A strong tree is cut down," the chapter adds. What looks strong is often only stiff.

**The soft overcomes the hard**

Chapter 78 uses [water](/glossary/water/) as proof: "Nothing in the world is softer or weaker than water, yet nothing beats it at attacking the hard and strong." Chapter 43 says: "The softest thing in the world rides over the hardest." And chapter 36: "The soft and weak overcome the hard and strong."

This is why softness goes together with [wu wei](/glossary/wu-wei/) and [not contending](/glossary/not-contending/). The soft does not push. It keeps flowing, and in time the hard gives way.

**Softness as strength**

Chapter 52 turns the usual idea around: "Holding to the soft is called strength." And chapter 10 asks: "Can you breathe soft as a newborn child?" Here softness is also something in the body and the breath, close to what the book says about [qi](/glossary/qi/).

**In daily life**

Most of us harden when we feel threatened: in an argument, under pressure, in fear. The book does not ask us to give in. It asks us to stay soft enough to listen and to change direction. See [The Tao Te Ching on Anger](/journal/tao-te-ching-on-anger/) for a simple practice.
