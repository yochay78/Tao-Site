**What tradition says**

The classic story comes from the Chinese historian Sima Qian, who wrote around 100 BCE. He describes Lao Tzu as a keeper of the archives at the Zhou court. When the dynasty declined, Lao Tzu left for the west. At the border pass, the gatekeeper asked him to write down his wisdom before he disappeared. He wrote a short book in two parts, about the Tao and about Te, and left. No one knows where he went.

**What historians say**

Sima Qian himself admitted he was unsure and offered other possible identities. Today, many scholars think the Tao Te Ching was gathered over time, and that "Lao Tzu" may be as much a name for the book's voice as for one man. The oldest known copies, written on bamboo slips and found in a tomb at Guodian in China, date to around 300 BCE.

**The voice in the book**

Whoever he was, the Lao Tzu of the text is a vivid presence. In chapter 20 he sounds almost lonely: "Others are bright. I am dim. Others are sharp. I am dull." In chapter 70 he says: "My words are easy to understand and easy to practice, yet no one in the world can understand or practice them."

You do not need to settle the history to read him. The book was always meant to be tested in your own life, not believed on authority.
