**A shocking line**

Chapter 5 opens with one of the hardest lines in the Tao Te Ching:

*Heaven and earth are not sentimental.*
*They treat the myriad things*
*like straw dogs.*

And then it says the same about the sage, whom this site calls [the Nameless](/glossary/the-nameless/): "He treats people like straw dogs." Many readers stop here and feel the book has turned cold.

**What straw dogs were**

The Chinese is 芻狗 (chu gou). Tradition says straw dogs were ritual figures, offerings made of woven straw. During the ceremony they were handled with respect. When it was over, they were thrown away. Scholars debate the details, but the point of the image is clear: honor while it lasts, no clinging after.

**Not cruel, but impartial**

[Heaven](/glossary/heaven/) and earth do not hate the things they let go. They simply do not hold on and do not play favorites. Rain falls on every field. Spring comes for everyone. Things arise, have their moment, and return, as chapter 16 says of [the myriad things](/glossary/myriad-things/).

**The bellows**

The chapter goes on with an image of [emptiness](/glossary/emptiness/): "Between heaven and earth, a bellows. Empty, yet it never runs dry." Because nature does not grip anything, it never runs out. And it ends: "Words wear out. Better to keep to the center."

**In daily life**

Being "not sentimental" in this sense can be a relief. You can care for people without owning them. You can give a project your full attention and then let it go. You can stop expecting life to treat you as a special case, and meet it as it is. This is close to [ziran](/glossary/ziran/), things being what they are by themselves.
