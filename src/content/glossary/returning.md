**The movement of the Tao**

Chapter 40 is only four lines, and it opens with this one:

*Returning is the movement of the Tao.*
*Weakness is the use of the Tao.*

The Tao does not march forward forever. It goes out and comes back, like breath, tides and seasons.

**Back to the root**

Chapter 16 shows it in nature: "The myriad things rise and fall and I watch them return. They flourish, and each goes back to its root." Then it adds: "Returning to the root is stillness." The root is not a loss. It is the source, the place new growth comes from.

**Far means returning**

Chapter 25 describes the Tao as great, and then: "Great means going on. Going on means far. Far means returning." The further something goes, the more it curves back toward its beginning.

**What we return to**

The book names several homes. Chapter 28: "you return to the infant," "to the boundless," and "to the uncarved block" ([pu](/glossary/pu/)). Chapter 52: "return to the mother" and "return to the brightness within." Chapter 14 says the Tao itself "returns to Nothing," the open [emptiness](/glossary/emptiness/) everything comes from.

**Two Chinese words**

*Fu* (復) means to come back. *Fan* (反), used in chapter 40, can mean both "return" and "reverse." Scholars debate which sense is stronger there, and many read both: the Tao brings things home and turns them into their opposite.

**In daily life**

When life circles back to the start, you have not fallen out of the Tao. Get still, go back to what is simple, and grow again from the root. See [How to Start Over](/journal/starting-over-tao/).
