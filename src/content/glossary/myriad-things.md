**Everything that exists**

The Chinese is 萬物 (wanwu), literally "ten thousand things." Ten thousand was a way of saying countless. So the phrase simply means everything: every person, tree, cloud, thought and event. This site's translation says "the myriad things."

**Born from the Tao**

Chapter 1 says: "The Nameless, root of heaven and earth. The named, mother of myriad things." Chapter 42 gives the famous sequence: [the Tao](/glossary/tao/) gives birth to [one](/glossary/the-one/), one to two, two to three, "Three gives birth to the myriad things." The next line adds that they "carry yin and embrace yang," so every thing holds [yin and yang](/glossary/yin-and-yang/) together.

**Rising and returning**

Chapter 16 shows their movement:

*The myriad things rise and fall*
*and I watch them return.*
*They flourish,*
*and each goes back to its root.*

This is [returning](/glossary/returning/). Nothing stays at its peak forever, and nothing is lost to the source.

**How the Tao treats them**

The Tao serves the myriad things without owning them. Chapter 8: "Water serves myriad things and never competes." Chapter 5 says heaven and earth treat them "like [straw dogs](/glossary/straw-dogs/)," without favorites. And chapter 76 notes that plants and trees "are soft and tender in life, dry and brittle in death."

**In daily life**

Seeing yourself as one of the myriad things can be calming. You are not outside the world, managing it. You rise, flourish and return like everything else. See [How to Start Over](/journal/starting-over-tao/) for chapter 16 and the root.
