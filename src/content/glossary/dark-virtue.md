**The quietest kind of goodness**

Most goodness wants to be noticed. We help, and we hope someone saw. We give, and part of us keeps a receipt. The Tao Te Ching points to a different kind, and it calls it 玄德 (xuan de), dark virtue.

Chapter 10 ends with its definition:

*To bear and to nourish,*
*to make and not to own,*
*to give and not to hold,*
*to lead and not to rule.*
*This is the dark virtue.*

**Why "dark"**

玄 (xuan) means dark, deep, mysterious. It is the same word chapter 1 uses for the source of everything: "Darkness within darkness, the gate to everything wonderful." Dark virtue is not shadowy or bad. It is deep, like a well you cannot see the bottom of. It works out of sight.

**Like the Tao itself**

Chapter 51 shows where this virtue comes from. The Tao gives birth to all things, and [te](/glossary/te/) nurtures them, "raises, shelters, ripens, and protects." Then: "Gives birth and does not possess. Acts and does not depend. Leads and does not rule. This is called deep virtue." This is how [the Mother](/glossary/the-mother/) of all things behaves. A person with dark virtue simply does the same on a human scale.

**Far and deep**

Chapter 65 adds that deep virtue "is far and deep. It turns things back, until they reach the great harmony." It does not push things forward by force. It brings them back to their own nature, close to [returning](/glossary/returning/).

**In daily life**

A parent who raises a child and then lets the child become someone else. A teacher whose students forget where they learned it. A leader whose people say, as chapter 17 puts it, "we did it ourselves." Help, and let go of the credit. This is [wu wei](/glossary/wu-wei/) applied to love and work.
