**Virtue that does not try**

If the Tao is the source and flow of all things, Te is what that looks like when it lives in someone. The word is often translated as "virtue," but it also carries the sense of power, potency or character: the way a seed has the power to become a tree.

Chapter 38 opens with a surprise:

*High virtue does not try to be virtuous,*
*and so it has virtue.*
*Low virtue never lets go of virtue,*
*and so it has none.*

In other words, real goodness is not a performance. The moment it becomes a rule to follow or an image to protect, something has already been lost. The chapter goes on: "When the Tao is lost, there is virtue. When virtue is lost, there is kindness. When kindness is lost, there is duty. When duty is lost, there is ritual."

**Deep virtue**

Chapter 51 gives the clearest picture: the Tao gives birth, virtue nurtures. It "raises, shelters, ripens, and protects." And then: "Gives birth and does not possess. Acts and does not depend. Leads and does not rule. This is called deep virtue."

**Where the word sits in the book**

The book's title joins both words: Tao and Te. By tradition, chapters 1 to 37 are called the Tao section and chapters 38 to 81 the Te section. On this site, Te is translated as "virtue" in the chapter texts.
