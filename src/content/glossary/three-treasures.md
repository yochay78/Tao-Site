**Three things worth guarding**

Most of the Tao Te Ching tells you what to let go of. Chapter 67 is one of the few places where Lao Tzu says what he keeps:

*I have three treasures that I hold and guard.*
*The first is compassion.*
*The second is thrift.*
*The third is not daring to be first in the world.*

The Chinese phrase san bao simply means "three treasures." Later Taoist traditions used the same phrase for other sets of three, so it helps to remember that here it means these three qualities.

**Each treasure opens a door**

The chapter does not leave them as rules. It shows what each one makes possible:

*Compassionate, so I can be brave.*
*Thrifty, so I can be generous.*
*Not daring to be first, so I can lead.*

Each pair sounds backwards. That is the point. Real courage grows out of care, not out of toughness. Real generosity grows out of not wasting, not out of having a lot. Real leadership grows out of not shoving to the front.

**What goes wrong without them**

Then comes the warning: "Now people abandon compassion and seek courage. Abandon thrift and seek abundance. Abandon being behind and seek to be ahead. This is death." We often want the fruit without the root: bravery without care, plenty without restraint, status without humility.

**Compassion first**

The chapter ends by returning to the first treasure: "Compassion wins in battle and holds in defense." Of the three, compassion is the one Lao Tzu puts in front, and the one he comes back to.

**In daily life**

Before a hard talk, start from care. Before a purchase or a yes, ask whether you are spending what you will need later. Before pushing your idea forward, try walking a step behind. These are small, ordinary moves, and they are exactly what chapter 67 is pointing to.
