**A person, not a title**

Almost every chapter of the Tao Te Ching that gives advice ends up describing one figure: the sage, in Chinese shengren (聖人). The sage is not a ruler, a priest or a famous teacher. The sage is the book's picture of a person whose life runs with the Tao instead of against it.

**Why "the Nameless"**

This site's translation calls the sage "the Nameless." The word echoes chapter 1: "The Nameless, root of heaven and earth." In the Chinese these are two different words, but the translation lets them speak to each other. The sage does not live for a name, a title or credit. Like the Tao, the sage works quietly underneath things.

**What the sage does**

Chapter 2 gives the clearest portrait:

*So the Nameless acts without striving*
*and teaches without words.*
*...*
*He works and does not cling.*
*The work is done and he does not stay.*

Chapter 7 adds the paradox: "So the Nameless steps back and ends up ahead." And chapter 64 shows the sage's patience: he "does not grasp, and so does not lose."

**Not doing less, doing it lightly**

The sage is busy. He leads, plans, finishes things and helps people. Chapter 63 says "even the Nameless treats things as hard, and in the end nothing is hard for him." What is missing is not effort but forcing, ego and the need to be seen. That is wu wei lived by a person.

**A mirror, not a statue**

Tradition attributes the book to Lao Tzu, but the text never says the sage is him. The sage works best as a mirror: when you read a line about the Nameless, ask where in your own day you could step back, let go of the credit, or stop one moment earlier.
