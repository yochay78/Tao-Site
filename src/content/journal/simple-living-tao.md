The Tao Te Ching says simplicity means wanting less, not just owning less. Chapter 19 puts it in four short lines: "Show the plain. Embrace the uncarved. Want little. Own little." When you stop chasing more, the ordinary things in your life become enough again.

Most of us feel it. Too many tabs open, too many things in the closet, too many plans for one weekend. We try to fix it by buying an organizer, downloading a new app, starting a big decluttering project. And somehow life gets fuller, not lighter.

Lao Tzu would smile at that. You cannot add your way to simplicity.

**What chapter 19 actually says**

Chapter 19 begins with something surprising: give up sageliness, give up cleverness, give up profit. Then it says these words alone are not enough, and gives this instead, in this site's translation:

*So let this be what you hold:*
*Show the plain. Embrace the uncarved.*
*Want little. Own little.*

"The uncarved" is the uncarved block, [pu](/glossary/pu/) in Chinese: wood before anyone has cut it into a shape. It is you before all the roles, the image and the extra layers. Simplicity, here, is not a look. It is going back to that plain, natural state.

Notice the order, too. "Want little" comes before "own little." The book does not start with your things. It starts with your wanting.

**Too much dulls the senses**

Chapter 12 explains why more rarely satisfies:

*The five colors blind the eye.*
*The five sounds deafen the ear.*
*The five flavors dull the tongue.*

This was written long before screens, but it describes them well. When everything is loud, bright and exciting, we stop really seeing anything. The tenth video feels like nothing. The third dessert tastes like nothing.

The chapter goes on: "Racing and hunting drive the heart mad. Rare treasures make the feet stumble." The chase itself makes us restless, and the things we chase make us trip.

**The belly, not the eye**

Then comes the turn:

*So the Nameless*
*looks to the belly and not the eye.*
*He lets go of that*
*and takes this.*

The eye always wants the next thing. The belly wants to be fed and then it is satisfied. The sage chooses what truly nourishes over what only excites. "That" is the endless show. "This" is what is right in front of you.

A simple test for any purchase, plan or habit: is this for the belly or for the eye? Will it feed my life, or only stimulate it for a moment?

**Every day, something is dropped**

Chapter 48 offers the method, and it is gentle:

*In pursuit of learning, every day something is added.*
*In pursuit of the Tao, every day something is dropped.*

You don't need a weekend of throwing things away. You need one thing a day. One unneeded purchase you don't make. One commitment you politely decline. One worry you put down. Less and less, without drama. This is the same spirit as [wu wei](/glossary/wu-wei/): not forcing, just no longer adding.

**Food sweet, home peaceful**

Chapter 80 paints the picture of a simple life. It describes a small country where people have tools and boats but rarely need them, and then:

*Let them find their food sweet,*
*their clothes lovely,*
*their homes peaceful,*
*their customs a joy.*

Nothing in that list is rare or expensive. The food is ordinary. The clothes are ordinary. What changed is the person eating and wearing them. When wanting quiets down, ordinary things taste sweet again.

This is close to what the book calls thrift, one of the [three treasures](/glossary/three-treasures/) in chapter 67: "Thrifty, so I can be generous." Living simply is not being stingy. It leaves you with more to give.

**A small practice for today**

1. Before you buy, open or say yes to something today, pause for one breath and ask: belly or eye?
2. Drop one thing. Only one: a purchase, an app, a plan, a habit.
3. At one meal, eat slowly, with no screen, and taste the food. Let it be sweet.

That is enough for today. Tomorrow you drop one more thing. Simplicity grows by itself once you stop feeding the opposite.
