"A journey of a thousand miles begins with a single step" comes from chapter 64 of the Tao Te Ching. It means that every great thing starts small and close at hand, so the way forward is not to stare at the whole road but to take the step in front of you.

It is one of the most quoted lines in the world. It is also one of the most misread. Most people hear it as a pep talk: just get going. Lao Tzu is saying something quieter, and more useful.

**The line in its place**

Here is the passage in this site's translation of chapter 64:

*A tree too big to embrace grows from a tiny shoot.*
*A tower of nine levels starts with a heap of earth.*
*A journey of a thousand miles begins under your feet.*

Notice the last words. Not "with a single step," but "under your feet." The original Chinese line ends with the words "beneath the feet." The English "single step" is a later paraphrase, and it shifts the meaning a little. A step is something you have to work up the courage to take. What is under your feet is already there.

The journey does not start somewhere far away, after you feel ready. It starts on the ground you are standing on right now.

**Big things are made of small things**

All three images in chapter 64 say the same thing. The tree, the tower and the journey are huge, but none of them began huge. A shoot. A heap of earth. A patch of ground.

Chapter 63 says it even more plainly:

*The hardest things in the world begin as easy.*
*The greatest things begin as small.*

This is not motivation. It is observation. Look at anything that worked in your life: a skill, a friendship, a home. It was built out of many small, ordinary moments, most of which did not feel important.

**Why we put things off**

Procrastination is usually not laziness. It is looking at the whole thousand miles at once. The mind sees the full size of the task, feels the weight of it, and freezes.

Chapter 63 gives the cure:

*Plan the difficult while it is easy.*
*Do the great while it is small.*

So make the first move small. Not "write the report," but "open the file and write one sentence." Not "get fit," but "put on your shoes." If even that feels heavy, make it smaller. The step should feel almost too easy. That is how you know it is the right size.

**Act early, before it grows**

Chapter 64 opens with lines that are easy to skip:

*What is at rest is easy to hold.*
*What has not yet shown is easy to plan for.*
*Act before it exists.*
*Set things in order before disorder.*

This is the other side of starting small. Problems also grow from tiny shoots. A short honest talk today is easier than a big fight next month. Five minutes of tidying now is easier than a lost weekend later. The wise move is often the small, early one that nobody notices.

**Don't grab, and don't quit near the end**

The same chapter warns: "One who grasps, loses." Starting small is not a trick to get to the finish faster. If you clutch at the result, you start pushing again, and the old tension returns.

And then comes a line many readers miss:

*People often fail when close to completion.*
*Be as careful at the end as at the beginning,*
*and you will not fail.*

Starting is hard. But finishing with the same calm attention you had at the start is just as rare. The journey is made of single steps all the way through, not only the first one.

**A small practice for today**

Pick one thing you have been putting off. Ask: what is the smallest move I could make on this in the next five minutes? Do only that. Then stop, and notice how it feels to have begun.

Tomorrow, do it again. That is the whole method. A tree too big to embrace was once a tiny shoot.
