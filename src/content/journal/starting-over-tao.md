The Tao Te Ching sees starting over as returning, not as failing. Chapter 40 opens with "Returning is the movement of the Tao": everything grows, reaches its fullness and goes back to its root, and that going back is where new life begins.

Most of us hate starting over. A job ends, a relationship ends, a plan falls apart, and it feels like all the years were wasted. We look at the empty ground and think: I am back at zero.

Lao Tzu would gently disagree. Back is not the same as nowhere.

**Returning is how the Tao moves**

Chapter 40 is only four lines long. Here it is in this site's translation:

*Returning is the movement of the Tao.*
*Weakness is the use of the Tao.*
*All things under heaven are born from being.*
*Being is born from non-being.*

Look at nature and you see it everywhere. The tide goes out and comes back. The tree drops its leaves and buds again. The day ends in night and night turns into morning. Nothing in nature moves in a straight line forever. It moves out, and then it comes home.

So when your life circles back, you have not fallen out of the Tao. You are moving the way the Tao moves.

**Each thing goes back to its root**

Chapter 16 says the same thing more slowly:

*The myriad things rise and fall*
*and I watch them return.*
*They flourish,*
*and each goes back to its root.*

*Returning to the root is stillness.*

Notice the order. First things flourish. Then they return. The return is not a punishment for flourishing. It is the next part of the same cycle.

And the root is not a loss. The root is what was holding you up the whole time. Your values, your care, the things you know in your body: none of that disappeared when the plan fell apart. You are back at the root, and the root is alive.

**Worn out, then renewed**

Chapter 22 has a line that is easy to skip:

*Be hollow, and you will be filled.*
*Be worn, and you will be renewed.*

We usually try to avoid being hollow and worn. But a cup can only be filled when it is empty. A field is planted after the old crop is cleared. Feeling emptied out after an ending is not proof that something is wrong. It can be the space where the new thing will grow. (Chapter 11 makes the same point about [emptiness](/glossary/emptiness/): the empty space inside a pot is what makes it useful.)

**Don't rebuild everything at once**

When we start over, we often panic. We want a new plan, a new identity and a new life by next week. That rush is just more pushing.

Chapter 16 begins with a different instruction: "Reach the utmost emptiness. Hold fast to stillness." Before you build, get quiet. Let the dust settle. You cannot see where to step while you are still spinning.

Chapter 52 adds: "Know the children, and return to the mother." When life gets crowded with affairs, go back to the source. Ask the simple questions again. What do I actually care about? What is true for me now, not five years ago?

**Weakness is the use of the Tao**

The second line of chapter 40 matters here too. "Weakness is the use of the Tao." Starting over usually feels weak. You are new again, unsure again, a beginner again.

The Tao Te Ching does not see that as a problem. A seedling is soft. A beginner is open. Being weak in this sense means you are not yet hardened into one shape, so you can still grow in any direction. (We wrote more about this in [Why the Soft Overcomes the Hard](/journal/why-the-weakest-wins/).)

**A small practice for today**

If you are in a season of starting over, try this:

1. Sit for two minutes and do nothing. Just breathe. That is your return to stillness.
2. Name one thing that is already gone, and say quietly: "That part is finished."
3. Name one thing that is still here: something you value, someone who cares, something you know how to do. That is your root.
4. Choose one small step that grows from that root. Only one.

You don't need the whole new life today. You need to come home, get still, and let the next shoot appear. Chapter 64 reminds us that "a journey of a thousand miles begins under your feet" (see [The Journey of a Thousand Miles](/journal/journey-of-a-thousand-miles/)).
