The Tao Te Ching says anger is not strength. Chapter 68 puts it in one short line: "A good fighter is not angry." The book's way with anger is not to push it down but to stay soft, stop contending, and let it pass before you act.

We all know the moment. Someone says the wrong thing, and heat rises in the chest. The words are out before we choose them. Later, when it cools down, we often wish we had said something else, or nothing at all.

Lao Tzu does not tell us we are bad for feeling angry. He simply points out what anger does to us.

**What chapter 68 actually says**

Chapter 68 is short. In this site's translation:

*A good soldier is not violent.*
*A good fighter is not angry.*
*A good conqueror does not engage.*
*A good employer stays beneath.*
*This is the virtue of not contending.*

Notice who is speaking here. Not a monk on a mountain, but someone thinking about soldiers and fighters. Even in a fight, the book says, anger is a weakness. The angry fighter is easy to read and easy to provoke. Whoever can make him angry can move him.

The calm one is harder to push. That is what the book means by [not contending](/glossary/not-contending/): not that you never stand your ground, but that you don't let the fight take you over.

**Hard and stiff, soft and alive**

Chapter 76 gives a picture you can feel in your own body:

*A person is soft and weak at birth,*
*hard and stiff at death.*
*So the hard and stiff are followers of death.*
*The soft and weak are followers of life.*

Anger is a hardening. The jaw tightens, the shoulders rise, the voice gets sharp. In that state we cannot listen, and we cannot change direction. "A strong tree is cut down," the chapter says. What cannot bend, breaks.

[Softness](/glossary/softness/) is not giving in. It is staying alive and flexible in the moment, like a young plant in the wind.

**Do not fight, and no one can blame you**

Chapter 8 compares the highest good to [water](/glossary/water/), which "serves myriad things and never competes." It ends with a simple rule:

*Do not fight,*
*and no one can blame you.*

Think of the last argument you regret. Usually the regret is not about what the other person did. It is about what we did once we were angry. When we don't add fuel, there is much less to clean up afterwards.

**The bitterness that remains**

Chapter 79 speaks to what happens after:

*After a great quarrel,*
*some bitterness always remains.*
*How can that be called good?*

Even when a fight is "settled," something stays behind. A heaviness. A list of who said what. The chapter's answer is surprising: the sage "keeps the left half of the tally and does not demand payment from others." In ancient China a tally was a contract cut in two, with each side keeping half. The sage holds his part, but he does not go around collecting.

In other words, he stops keeping score. Not because the other person was right, but because carrying the bill is too heavy.

**Small, while it is easy**

Chapter 63 offers a practical key: "Plan the difficult while it is easy. Do the great while it is small." Anger is easiest to meet in its first second, as a little heat, before it becomes words. A big blow-up almost always starts as a small irritation that nobody noticed.

The same chapter has a short, hard line: "Repay hatred with virtue." Not with more hatred. With [Te](/glossary/te/), the quiet power that comes from being in tune with the Tao.

**A small practice for the next time anger rises**

1. Notice the heat as early as you can. Name it silently: "anger."
2. Soften one thing in the body: the jaw, the hands or the shoulders. Let one breath go out slowly.
3. Wait before you answer. Even ten seconds is enough for the wave to begin passing.
4. Then speak, if you still need to, from the calm place underneath.

You are not pushing the anger away. You are just not obeying it. Like weather, it comes and it goes, and you are the sky it moves through.

If you want to grow that steady place inside, so that what others say does not knock you over so easily, the book below is about exactly that.
